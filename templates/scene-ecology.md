# Scene ecology — <chapter>

> Writer-facing material/social field. Describe a world already operating, not events that must happen.

## Place and material world
Give concrete scene-relevant reality: spatial use, work surfaces, stock/tools/materials, money or paperwork, transport, weather/heat/noise where relevant, objects that are actually in circulation, and practical limits on movement or attention.

Do not use this section to stage a clue.

## Ongoing processes
Describe the few processes that would continue even if the main plot did not advance today: work cycles, household tasks, buying/selling, preparation, cleanup, transport, waiting, visitors, storage, food, money, or other chapter-specific activity.

Use enough specificity that the Writer can imagine how a stretch of time passes, but do not prescribe a micro-sequence.

## Competing attention
What keeps pulling present people away from a single clean topic? Include ordinary priorities, unfinished work, bodily needs, timing, inconvenience, family demands or social obligations that naturally coexist.

## Social field
Describe who can plausibly enter, linger, leave, overhear, interrupt or retell things because of their own business and relationships. Preserve incomplete hearing, bad memory, boredom and topic drift as normal social conditions.

## Horror contact surface
State the physical/material boundary through which approved supernatural pressure can touch the scene. Describe what reality permits, not how the Writer must stage or explain it.

## Local invention
Transient customers, errands, temporary moods, small work problems, misunderstandings and chapter-local events are free when consistent with the above field. Record only inventions that create later durable obligations.
